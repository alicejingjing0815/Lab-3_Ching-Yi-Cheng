Lab 3: Linear Regression: Model Specification, Comparison, and Interpretation

Complaint Handling, Learning Opportunities, and Performance-Based Raises as Predictors of Overall Workplace Ratings

Author

Ching-Yi Cheng

Published

October 30, 2026

Introduction

Employees’ overall workplace ratings may be associated with how their organization handles complaints, provides learning opportunities, and awards pay increases based on performance. This report examines whether learning opportunities and performance-based raises explain additional variation in overall ratings beyond complaint handling alone.

Workflow

The analysis follows a complete linear regression workflow: Explore Data → Specify Models → Estimate Models → Compare Models → Evaluate Assumptions → Select a Model → Interpret Results

Overview of the Models

Four nested regression models are compared to examine whether learning opportunities and performance-based raises explain additional variation in overall workplace ratings beyond complaint handling.

Model Formula Purpose
M0: Null rating ~ 1 Establish a baseline using only the mean overall rating
M1: Complaints rating ~ complaints Examine the association between complaint handling and overall ratings
M2: + Learning rating ~ complaints + learning Assess whether learning opportunities explain additional variation beyond complaint handling
M3: + Raises rating ~ complaints + learning + raises Assess whether performance-based raises explain further variation beyond complaint handling and learning

The models are nested: each successive model retains all predictors from the preceding model and adds one predictor.

Dataset

R Core Team. (n.d.). The Chatterjee–Price attitude data. R documentation. https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/datasets/html/attitude.html

1. Load packages and prepare the setup

Keep the same complete-case sample for every model

The analysis includes 30 departments.

Research Question and Prediction

How are favorable evaluations of complaint handling, learning opportunities, and performance-based raises associated with overall workplace ratings? Do learning opportunities and performance-based raises explain additional variation beyond complaint handling alone?

I expect favorable evaluations of complaint handling to be positively associated with overall workplace ratings, because effective responses to employee concerns may reflect a supportive work environment. I also expect favorable evaluations of learning opportunities and performance-based raises to explain additional variation in overall ratings, as these aspects may reflect opportunities for development and recognition of employee contributions.

4. Compare the models

We evaluate the models using two complementary approaches.

Nested-model F-tests determine whether the additional predictor(s) in a larger model produce a statistically significant improvement in fit.

Overall model performance describes how well each model fits the observed data.

Our main performance metrics are:

  • R²: Proportion of variation explained.
  • Adjusted R²: Explained variation adjusted for model complexity.
  • RMSE: Root mean squared error, expressed in rating-score units.
  • ΔR²: Increase in explained variation compared with the preceding model.

4.1. Conduct nested-model comparisons

Adding complaint handling significantly improved model fit relative to the intercept-only model (p < .001). Adding learning opportunities did not significantly improve fit beyond complaint handling (p = .135), and performance-based raises provided no significant further improvement (p = .877).

Each row represents a model. The F-statistic and p-value on each row after the first test whether that model improves fit relative to the preceding model.

For example:

  • M1 versus M0 tests the contribution of complaint handling.
  • M2 versus M1 tests the additional contribution of learning opportunities beyond complaint handling.
  • M3 versus M2 tests the additional contribution of performance-based raises beyond complaint handling and learning opportunities.

Because the rows in glances and tests represent the same models in the same order, we can combine them using cbind().

Next, calculate the change in R² between successive models using lag(). We will also calculate RMSE directly from each model’s residuals.

Why calculate RMSE separately?

Although glance() provides a statistic called sigma, this is the residual standard error rather than the ordinary in-sample RMSE. The two use different denominators.

For consistency, we calculate RMSE explicitly as the square root of the mean squared residual.

Interpreting the combined table

M3 explained the largest proportion of variation in overall workplace ratings (R² = 0.7083), although its advantage over M2 was negligible. Adjusted R² increased from 0.67 in M1 to 0.686 in M2, then decreased to 0.675 in M3. Adding complaint handling explained 68.1% of the total variation and significantly improved fit (p < .001), while reducing RMSE from 11.97 to 6.76 percentage points—a substantial improvement relative to the baseline. Adding learning opportunities explained a further 2.67 percentage points of variance, but the improvement was not statistically significant (p = 0.135). Its RMSE reduction of 0.29 percentage points appears modest. Adding performance-based raises explained only another 0.03 percentage points of variance (p = 0.876) and reduced RMSE by just 0.003 percentage points, an improvement that appears practically negligible. Overall, complaint handling provided the clearest explanatory contribution; learning offered a modest but uncertain additional contribution, whereas raises added little beyond the other predictors.

5. Select a provisional model

M2, which includes complaint handling and learning opportunities, was provisionally selected because it had the highest adjusted R² and a slightly lower RMSE than M1. However, adding learning did not significantly improve model fit, so M1 remains a plausible, simpler alternative. M3 offered negligible additional explained variance and a lower adjusted R² than M2, providing little justification for including raises. Diagnostic findings will inform the final model selection.

The provisionally selected model is M2: + Learning.

Its performance is:

  • R² = 0.708
  • Adjusted R² = 0.686
  • In-sample RMSE = 6.47 percentage points of favourable overall workplace ratings.

We next examine the model’s diagnostic plots and multicollinearity to assess potential limitations.

6. Evaluate regression diagnostics

The residuals-versus-fitted plot showed no pronounced curvature, suggesting that the linear specification was broadly reasonable. However, the Q–Q plot showed departures from normality, and the scale–location plot suggested uneven residual variability, particularly greater variability at lower fitted values. Department 24 had relatively high leverage and warrants further inspection, although no observations exceeded the displayed Cook’s distance = .5 contours.

Multicollinearity was low, with VIF values of 1.55 for both predictors. These findings do not rule out M2, but the small sample and potential heteroscedasticity warrant caution when interpreting conventional p-values and confidence intervals, particularly for the uncertain additional contribution of learning.

7. Select and report the preferred model

7.1. Model selection

M2 was selected as the preferred model because it had the highest adjusted R² and a modestly lower RMSE than M1, while allowing the association between learning opportunities and overall ratings to be examined after accounting for complaint handling. However, the additional contribution of learning was small and did not reach statistical significance, so M1 remains a defensible, simpler alternative. M3 was not selected because raises contributed negligible additional explained variance and reduced adjusted R². Diagnostics for M2 showed no pronounced curvature and low multicollinearity, but suggested departures from residual normality, uneven residual variability, and a high-leverage observation. M2 is therefore retained with caution, particularly regarding the uncertain contribution of learning.

7.3. Interpreting the coefficients

Holding favorable evaluations of learning opportunities constant, a one-percentage-point increase in favorable evaluations of complaint handling was associated with a 0.64-percentage-point increase in predicted favorable overall workplace ratings, 95% CI [0.4, 0.89], p < .001. The confidence interval excluded zero, supporting a positive conditional association.

Holding favorable evaluations of complaint handling constant, a one-percentage-point increase in favorable evaluations of learning opportunities was associated with an estimated 0.21-percentage-point increase in predicted favorable overall ratings, 95% CI [-0.06, 0.49], p = 0.128. This interval included zero, indicating uncertainty about the direction and magnitude of learning’s conditional association. These conventional confidence intervals and p-values should be interpreted cautiously given the diagnostic concerns.

7.4. Overall model performance

The selected model, M2: + Learning, explained approximately 70.8% of the variation in department-level overall workplace ratings (R² = 0.708). Its adjusted R² was 0.686, accounting for model complexity. Its in-sample RMSE was 6.47 percentage points, describing the root-mean-square discrepancy between observed and fitted percentages of favorable overall ratings. These statistics describe fit within the current sample and do not establish predictive accuracy for new departments.

8. Discussion and limitations

Complaint handling showed the clearest association with overall workplace ratings. Departments with more favorable evaluations of complaint handling tended to receive higher overall ratings, even after accounting for learning opportunities. Learning showed a positive estimated association, but its confidence interval included zero and its addition produced only a modest improvement in model fit. Performance-based raises added almost no explanatory value beyond complaint handling and learning. Thus, these data provide stronger evidence for the contribution of complaint handling than for the additional contributions of learning or raises.

Several limitations qualify these conclusions. The observational design does not establish causality, and department-level associations cannot be assumed to describe individual employees. The sample contained only 30 departments from one organization, limiting precision and generalizability. The measures came from the same survey, so shared response tendencies may partly explain their associations. Diagnostic plots also suggested departures from residual normality, uneven residual variability, and a high-leverage observation, warranting caution when interpreting conventional confidence intervals and p-values. Finally, incremental contributions depend on the order in which predictors were entered, and in-sample fit does not establish predictive accuracy for new departments. The uncertain findings for learning and raises therefore should not be interpreted as evidence that these workplace features are unimportant.
